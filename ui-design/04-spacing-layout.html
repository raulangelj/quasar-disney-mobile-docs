Decision 4 — Spacing & layout

Palette (A) and typeface (Inter, locked ladder) are identical in all three mocks. Only the spacing values change. Each phone renders the same content — two continue-watching tiles, a metadata block, and a portrait row — so what you're comparing is how much of the storefront survives one viewport, which is the practical meaning of density in a carousel app.

The finding that constrains all three. Density is a visual choice; touch targets are not. The reference's ⋮ overflow glyph and the password eye toggle are ~20 pt of ink, well under the 44×44 pt iOS HIG / 48×48 dp Android minimum that WCAG 2.1 AA also expects. The dashed cyan squares in each mock are that 44 pt box drawn to scale. Note it stays the same size in the tight option and the airy one: visual density and hit density are separate, and the fix is hitSlop / a padded pressable wrapper, not a bigger icon. This is a component-spec rule (decision 6), recorded here because it is what stops the tight option from being an accessibility problem.

Option 1 — Reference-tight

recommended 4 pt base · gutter 16

The transcribed reference values: 16 pt screen gutter, 8 pt between tiles, 20 pt between row groups. Roughly 2.5 rows per viewport — a storefront that reads as full, which is the whole visual argument of a carousel home. Tradeoff: tight vertical rhythm leaves less margin for error when Spanish strings run long, and it depends entirely on the hit-area rule above being honored.

Option 2 — Comfortable

8 pt base · gutter 24

An 8-pt rhythm throughout: 24 pt gutter, 12 pt tile gap, 32 pt between groups, taller 56 pt fields. Calmer, more premium, and the most forgiving of long translated strings. Tradeoff: under two rows survive the viewport, so the storefront reads as a list of things rather than a wall of content — and on a demo whose subject is a Disney+-like storefront, that reads as a different product.

Option 3 — Middle

4 pt base · gutter 20

Splits the difference: 20 pt gutter, 12 pt tile gap, 24 pt groups, 52 pt fields. Slightly more air than the reference without losing the third row. Tradeoff: it is a compromise nobody asked for — it neither matches the reference values a stakeholder could hold up side by side, nor gains the clear breathing room of option 2. Included because it is the honest middle, not because I'd pick it.

The spacing scale — same in every option

confirm, don't choose

A 4 pt base scale with named steps, regardless of which density you pick. The density options above differ in which steps they use, not in the ladder itself: option 1 reaches for space.4/space.5 where option 2 reaches for space.6/space.8. A strict 8-pt-only scale was rejected because the reference's real values include 8, 12, and 20 — an 8-only grid would force rounding every one of them.

1 · 4
2 · 8
3 · 12
4 · 16
5 · 20
6 · 24
8 · 32
10 · 40
12 · 48
16 · 64

Naming. Steps are named by multiplier (space[4] === 16), not by role (space.gutter). Role aliases get added on top for the three values a component author should never re-derive: layout.gutter, layout.tileGap, layout.rowGap. That keeps criterion A2 ("zero hardcoded spacing") checkable — any raw number in a component is a violation, with no argument about whether it "counts".

Not in this decision: safe-area insets (notch, home indicator) and orientation are decision 10; the 44 pt minimum target and focus rings are decision 11. The tab bar's own height is fixed by platform convention, not by this scale (decision 15).
